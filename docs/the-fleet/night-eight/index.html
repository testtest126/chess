A Board With More Room

Go is played on a flat 19×19 grid, and has been for about as long as anyone's kept score. HyperGo asks the small, slightly absurd question of what a legal game of Go even means once the board stops being flat — 3D, then 4D, then 5D, the same rules of liberty and capture holding at every step, because the engine underneath was never written to know how many dimensions it was in.

§ 1 — the leap

The rules never asked how many axes there were.

Every function in the engine takes a dimension count as a plain argument — not a global, not a mode. That one decision is the entire reason 4D and 5D turned out to be an extension, not a rewrite.

The first version was honest about being small: a standalone 3D prototype, deliberately kept out of the way of the published piece — its own commit says outright that it doesn't touch docs/index.html, breath.sh, or the workflow that keeps the real piece breathing. A point is just a coordinate array; a mixed‑radix index flattens it into one board buffer regardless of how many numbers are in that array. Liberties, groups, captures, suicide‑that‑captures — none of it cares whether it's counting neighbors in three dimensions or five.

Extending to 4D and 5D, the same day, changed nothing about that core — it only had to solve rendering. The trick is the same one “5D chess” uses: a 4D board becomes a grid of 2D boards, and a 5D board becomes a grid of those grids. Orthogonal neighbors fall out for free at every step — 6 in 3D, 8 in 4D, 10 in 5D — because 2×dimensions was always going to be the honest count for a board that only ever moves along its own axes.

§ 2 — the proof

A capture, verified across a dimension it didn't start in.

Thirty-six assertions passing is one kind of evidence. A real game, actually played, is a different and better kind.

01

Hunted down by its own liberties

5D · interactive, via CDP · DOM‑reconstructed

Past the self‑tests, a real 5D game was played through the actual UI: the weakest White group found by its true liberty points, hunted down move by move, board state reconstructed independently from the DOM after every click rather than trusted from the engine's own log. The capturing move and the stone it captured sat in different sub‑boards, differing along the fifth axis — confirming captures resolve correctly across a higher‑dimensional link, not just within one visible grid.

A dimension a human can't see into is still a dimension a bug can hide in. Play a real game there before calling it done.
02

Folded in, then given its own door

PR #1 · docs/hypergo.html · published, unlinked

The experiment merged into Palimpsest's main branch as pull request #1, and was published the same day at its own standalone page — reachable, but deliberately not linked from the piece's own front door. HyperGo sits alongside the thing that forgets on purpose; it doesn't get to speak for it.

Publishing something doesn't require making it the main event.

§ 3 — the room

More axes. Same liberty.

Nobody needed a five‑dimensional Go board. That was never the point — the point was finding out whether the rules of a 3,000‑year‑old game were actually about a flat grid, or about something underneath the grid that doesn't care how many directions a stone can be surrounded from. They weren't about the grid.

It's a side room, not a wing of the house. That's exactly the shape it was always meant to take.
